An AI draft can only be as accurate as what it reads. There are two ways to give it something to read: you **supply the sources**, or a search tool **finds them** from your brief. Neither is always better. Each fails in its own way, and knowing how is what lets you choose, or combine them.

## The two approaches

- **Sources you supply.** You pick the pages, and only those are read. The draft rests on evidence you chose and would cite yourself.
- **Automatic research.** Search queries are written from your brief, a search service returns pages, and the best results become the sources. You get coverage without having to look.

## How they compare

| | Sources you supply | Automatic research |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Control** | Complete: nothing you didn't choose | Partial: you review what was found |
| **Effort** | Finding 3–5 good pages takes time | Seconds |
| **Coverage** | Limited to what you know | Can surface sources you didn't know |
| **Freshness** | As current as the pages you pick | Can ask for current figures explicitly |
| **Typical failure** | Your blind spots and preferences become the article's | Search-optimised, affiliate or copied pages slip in |
| **Best for** | Client pages, regulated topics, local detail | Unfamiliar topics, breadth, filling gaps |

## When to supply your own

Choose the sources yourself when:

- **The facts belong to someone.** A client's products, prices, policies and terms should come from the client's own pages, not from a reseller's summary.
- **Mistakes are expensive.** For health, legal, financial and safety topics, start from official bodies, regulators and the primary documents.
- **You already know the authority.** If there is an obvious primary source, a search can only add noise around it.
- **The detail is local.** Timetables, opening hours and local rules are often best on small official sites that rank poorly.
- **The knowledge isn't online.** Interviews, your own tests and internal data have to be supplied; a search won't find them.

## When automatic research helps

Let search find sources when:

- **The topic is new to you**, and you don't yet know who the authorities are.
- **You need breadth**: several perspectives, or more examples than you have time to collect.
- **Figures change**, such as prices, statistics and rules, and the search can ask for this year's.
- **You've supplied a few pages** and want the rest of the article covered.

## The best of both

On most articles, combine them:

1. **Seed** the brief with the primary sources you know: the official page, the client's page, the original study.
2. **Let search fill the gaps** around them, up to a sensible total; five to eight sources is plenty for one article.
3. **Review the list before anything is written.** This is where weak sources are cheapest to remove.
4. **Check the claims after drafting** against the sources that survived.

## A source checklist

Before a page is allowed to support your article, ask:

- **Who published it?** An official body, the company itself, a recognised publication, or someone you can't identify?
- **Is it primary?** Does it state the fact, or repeat someone else's statement of it? Prefer the original.
- **How old is it?** Check the publication or update date, not just whether the page still loads.
- **Is it on topic?** Same product, place, period and audience as your article?
- **Would you link to it yourself?** If you wouldn't cite it in your own name, it shouldn't support a claim.

Drop aggregators, unsigned listicles and pages that exist mainly to rank. A shorter list of strong sources makes a better article than a long list of weak ones.
